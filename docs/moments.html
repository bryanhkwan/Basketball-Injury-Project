The moment players get hurt

Play-by-play never says why a player got hurt. It does record the second a player left the game for good. I used that moment to find the plays that come right before injuries across seven NBA seasons, then tested them in college basketball against a plan I wrote down before seeing any college results.

The question

Play-by-play says when, not why.

Injury reports say what got hurt, like an ankle or a hamstring, but almost never how. Play-by-play logs every shot, foul and substitution to the second, and it has no line for “rolled an ankle.”

So I looked for a footprint instead. When a player leaves a competitive game early, never comes back, plays well under their usual minutes and then misses the next game with a new injury, the injury most likely happened in the seconds before they left. I call that an injury exit.

  1. The half-minute beforeWhat was happening?

    Shots, drives, fouls, steals, rebounds and turnovers by the player, and what the other team did.

  2. The exitLeaves for good

    Taken out of a competitive game early, well under their usual minutes, and never returns.

  3. The next gameMisses it, injured

    Listed with a new injury on the NBA’s official report, or on a public injury page in college.

The method

Compare every player with themselves.

  1. Find the injury exits.

    For each of the 6,470 new injuries on the NBA’s official injury reports, 2019-20 to 2025-26, I read the play-by-play of the team’s game before it. 985 of them, about 1 in 7, ended in an injury exit. Players leave early for other reasons too: among new injuries right after a game the player played in, 17% followed an early exit like this, against 4% of games followed by a normal one, so about 79% of traced exits are tied to the injury rather than chance.

  2. Compare each one with the same player’s normal substitutions.

    For each injury exit I listed what happened in the 30 seconds before it, then did the same for that player’s ordinary substitutions that season. Comparing players only with themselves takes position, role and playing style out of the picture. In statistics terms, it’s a case-crossover design with Mantel–Haenszel odds ratios.

  3. Try to break every result.

    With 240 comparisons, some will look real by chance. A result had to survive a false-discovery correction (Benjamini–Hochberg), point the same way in 2019-22 and 2022-26, and hold up at the same kind of stop in play, against random moments on the floor, against other injuries, under tighter and looser definitions of an injury exit, and against early exits that weren’t followed by an injury.

  4. Then test it somewhere new.

    Before seeing any college results, I wrote down five NBA patterns and the bar the four main ones had to clear, saved a fingerprint of that plan, and only then ran it on Division I men’s basketball.

NBA results

Three plays held up.

The share of injury exits with each play in the 30 seconds before, against the same players’ normal substitutions. Hover over a row, or tab to it, for the details.

Being fouled and sent to the line holds up only for ankle injuries. For other injuries the rise disappears in the toughest check, which compares injury exits with random moments that end at the next dead ball, the way a hurt player gets off the floor. The steal result counts all injuries together, and most kinds lean the same way; it fits the sprint, finish or scramble that follows a steal. Rim defense is specific to hand, wrist, finger and arm injuries: the player was on defense for a layup, dunk or tip-in, though the play-by-play doesn’t say who contested it.

Timing

It happens in the last few seconds.

If these plays only marked a long, tiring shift, they would show up as often two minutes before the exit as 15 seconds before. They don’t. The link is strongest in the last 15 to 30 seconds and almost gone two minutes out, which points to the play itself.

The college test

Then I tried to break it in college.

College basketball is the harder test. There’s no league-wide injury report to download, so I rebuilt seven seasons of Covers’ public injury page from 191 saved copies on the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine, matched them to the NCAA’s own play-by-play, and found 328 injury exits.

The plan came first: five NBA patterns, the bar the four main ones had to clear (a Holm-adjusted p below 0.05), and the fifth as a side check with no bar. Here’s how each one came out next to the NBA.

  • 5 of 5leaned the same way as in the NBA.
  • 1 of 4main checks cleared the bar: pulled muscles after a drive or fast-break finish, on just 3 players, so it’s fragile.
  • 1.9×the odds with all five combined (likely 1.3 to 2.8), and 1.9× without the fragile one. A check added after the plan.

So college supports the NBA patterns as a group but doesn’t firmly establish any single one. The ankle check isn’t quite the same measure in both, either: the NBA’s play-by-play can only count fouls with free throws, while the college link came mostly from fouls with no free throws, at the whistle.

Signs

How a hurt player leaves.

Some patterns aren’t causes. They’re the footprint of an injury that just happened, and together they could flag an exit worth a look on film.

In college, 40% of injury exits came at a stop with no foul, timeout, turnover or violation logged, against 18% of normal substitutions: often the referees stopping play for a hurt player. In the NBA, 32% of injury exits came at the team’s own timeout, against 14%.

Coverage

What the play-by-play can see.

Sudden injuries show up most. In the NBA, broken bones, concussions and sprains are traced most often, and soreness that builds up over time least. Most injuries never end a player’s night early, so they leave no trace here at all.

Conclusions

What I found.

  1. Play-by-play can find the moment, about 1 in 7 times.

    985 of 6,470 new NBA injuries ended in an injury exit, and about 79% of those are tied to the injury rather than chance. That’s enough to study what was happening when players got hurt, even though no line in the data says so.

  2. Contact plays carry the signal.

    Three plays held up in every check: being fouled before ankle injuries (4.1× as often), a steal before injuries of any kind (2.4× as often), and defending a rim attack before hand and arm injuries (52% of exits against 32% of normal substitutions). Pulled muscles after a drive are a lead, and ankle injuries after a rebound aren’t proven.

  3. It’s the play, not the shift.

    The link is strongest in the last 15 to 30 seconds before the exit and almost gone two minutes out. That points to the play itself rather than fatigue building up over a long stretch.

  4. College points the same way, but doesn’t prove it.

    All five patterns leaned the same way in Division I, and one of the four main checks cleared the bar I set in advance, on just 3 players. Combined, the five patterns give an odds ratio of 1.9 (likely 1.3 to 2.8), and that holds without the fragile one.

  5. How a player leaves is a signal too.

    Being the only player taken out, leaving at a stop with nothing logged, a teammate shooting the player’s free throws, or the team spending its own timeout all come with injury exits more often than with normal substitutions. Together they could flag the exits worth a look on film.

  6. The scale is small.

    The three solid patterns account for about 60 injury exits in seven seasons beyond what you’d expect by chance, about 9 a season across the whole league. Most injuries happen out of view, in practice or building up over time, so prevention needs film, trainer records and load data, not just box scores.

What a team could do with this

  • Use injury exits as a film trigger. Pull the 30 seconds before every early exit and log what happened: a landing, contact, a hard cut, a collision.
  • Start prevention work where the data points. Contact on fouled drives and landings, the sprint and finish after a steal, and contesting at the rim are the places to look first.
  • Build the data the public can’t see. Athletic trainers’ injury logs (with the department’s approval), practice load and tracking data are what can turn these patterns into causes.

Limits

What the data can’t see.

  • The play-by-play only records who made each play and who was on the floor. It can’t show landings, collisions, cuts without the ball, or rebounds someone else won.
  • Most injuries never end a player’s night early, and injuries in practice or that build up over time don’t show up at all.
  • Injury exits are inferred, not observed: about 1 in 5 traced NBA exits are likely coincidence.
  • College injuries come from saved copies of a public injury page, which are sparse in 2022-23 and 2024-25. Old-format NCAA play-by-play, mostly from 2019-21, can’t show fouls drawn or drives.
  • The NBA patterns came out of many comparisons. The false-discovery correction and the college test guard against chance, but these are associations, not proof of cause.
